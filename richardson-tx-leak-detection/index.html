Testing a Richardson Backyard Pool Before Calling for Leak Detection

Pool leak detection starts with a simple DIY check — the bucket test — before any technician visit is needed. Fill a five-gallon bucket to within two inches of the rim, mark the water level inside the bucket and on the pool wall with waterproof tape, and let both sit for 24 hours with the pump off. If the pool loses more water than the bucket loses to evaporation, you have evidence of a structural or plumbing leak rather than normal surface loss.

Pinhole Leaks in Richardson Copper Pipes vs PEX Repiping

Plumbing leak detection in Richardson disproportionately involves copper pipe because the city's residential construction era — concentrated between the late 1950s and mid-1980s — predates the widespread adoption of flexible plastic supply lines. Copper was the professional standard, but it was not engineered to withstand 60 years of hard-water mineral attack from the inside and Blackland clay stress from the outside simultaneously.

How Pinhole Corrosion Develops

Hard water from the North Texas Municipal Water District deposits calcium and magnesium scale on the interior wall of copper pipe. Over time, micro-pitting forms beneath those deposits, and electrochemical corrosion eats through the wall from the inside — producing a pinhole leak that can deliver a steady drip into the slab fill material for months before it migrates to the surface. Electronic amplification devices, thermal imaging cameras, and tracer-gas injection are the tools that make it possible to locate these leaks without full excavation, keeping slab leaks repair scopes as narrow as possible.

For homes where multiple copper pipe pinhole leaks have occurred, or where a full slab leaks repair reveals widespread corrosion, repiping to PEX pipe is often more cost-effective over a ten-year horizon than repeated spot repairs. PEX pipe is cross-linked polyethylene — flexible, corrosion-immune, and tolerant of the freeze-thaw cycles Richardson experienced during the February 2021 winter storm. The standard DIY method for pool leak detection is called the bucket test, and it only takes about 24 hours to run. Here is how to do it step by step:

  • Step 1 — Fill a bucket: Fill a standard five-gallon bucket about three-quarters full with pool water and set it on the first or second step of your pool so it sits partially submerged.
  • Step 2 — Mark both water levels: Use waterproof tape or a marker to mark the water level inside the bucket and the water level on the pool wall at the same time.
  • Step 3 — Turn off the pump and wait 24 hours: Keep the pool unused and let both water levels sit undisturbed for a full day.
  • Step 4 — Compare the levels: Evaporation affects both the bucket and the pool equally. If the pool has lost significantly more water than the bucket, you likely have a leak rather than normal evaporation.
