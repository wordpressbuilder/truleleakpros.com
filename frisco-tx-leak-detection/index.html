Why Even New Construction in Frisco Gets Slab Leaks

A newly poured slab does not mean stable ground beneath it. Frisco's Blackland Prairie clay soils — documented extensively by the USDA Web Soil Survey for Collin County — undergo significant volume changes with every wet and dry cycle. A slab poured during a wet spring sits on swollen clay; by mid-summer, as Frisco averages over 230 days of sunshine per year and extended dry periods follow the spring thunderstorm season, the same clay contracts and the slab loses uniform support. Copper or PEX pipe embedded in the foundation flexes with each seasonal cycle, and micro-fractures can open within the first three to five years of construction.

Why Frisco's summer matters to your pipes: The extended dry spells from July through September represent the highest-risk window for slab pipe stress fractures. Homes that passed a pressure test in spring may show a leak by late summer — not because anything was missed, but because the soil has since moved. If you notice a warm floor patch or unexplained water bill increase in that window, book a detection visit before autumn rains rehydrate the clay and temporarily close the fracture.

Hard water from the North Texas Municipal Water District supply compounds the problem. NTMWD delivers water with moderate-to-high mineral hardness typical of North Texas surface sources, and those minerals accelerate internal corrosion inside copper pipe fittings and slab-embedded joints. The result is pinhole leaks — losses too small to hear without acoustic leak detection equipment but large enough to saturate the sand bed beneath the slab over weeks. Infrared thermal imaging is particularly effective here: a wet sand bed holds temperature differently from a dry one, and that contrast appears clearly on a thermal camera before any moisture reaches the surface.

Post-freeze conditions add a third failure mechanism. February 2021's Winter Storm Uri, documented in National Weather Service Fort Worth after-action reports, demonstrated that newer Frisco construction with PEX or copper supply lines routed through uninsulated exterior walls or slab penetrations is vulnerable to burst damage. Post-freeze slab penetrations and exterior-wall runs are the first sections our network of licensed pros inspect whenever a call comes in after a hard freeze event.

The acoustic and thermal detection process, step by step

Our standard detection sequence begins with a pressure test: we isolate the supply system and charge it to operating pressure, then monitor for a pressure drop over a timed interval. A confirmed drop tells us a leak is present and gives us a rough sense of its volume rate. We then walk the slab with acoustic leak detection equipment — ground microphones and electronic amplifiers that filter out ambient noise and lock onto the distinctive frequency signature of water escaping a pressurised pipe through a fracture in concrete.

Infrared thermal imaging follows as a secondary confirmation. Where acoustic signals suggest a failure zone, the thermal camera shows the temperature differential caused by moisture migration through the sand bed or concrete, narrowing the target area to within inches. Only after both methods agree on a location do we mark the slab for cutting — ensuring that the smallest possible area is disturbed, and that post-tension tendons are avoided entirely.

Finding Hidden Leaks in a Frisco Backyard Pool

Pool leaks in Frisco fall into two distinct categories — shell leaks and equipment pad leaks — and the detection method differs meaningfully between them. A shell leak originates at a crack or failed fitting in the pool structure itself; an equipment pad leak originates at a pump seal, valve, filter housing or return line fitting in the mechanical bay. Because equipment pad leaks are far more common and far easier to access, our network of licensed pros always inspect the equipment pad with a moisture meter and visual check before concluding the shell is at fault.

For shell leaks, the process begins with a pressure test on the return and suction plumbing runs to rule out underground pipe failure between the shell and the equipment pad. If the pressure holds, we move to a dye test at suspected fittings, skimmers and light niches to identify where water is escaping through the shell. Frisco's large irrigated lots mean that a slow shell leak can go unnoticed for weeks because the lawn is already wet and the pool auto-fill valve compensates for the loss — the pool level stays constant while water escapes continuously.

A quick check before calling us

Place a bucket of pool water on a step inside the pool, mark both the bucket water level and the pool water level with tape, and leave both undisturbed for 24 hours with the pump off. If the pool level drops significantly more than the bucket — which loses water only to evaporation — a leak is almost certainly present and a pressure test on the plumbing runs is the right next step.
