Slab Leak Detection for Gainesville's Older Foundations

Slab leaks are among the most consequential plumbing failures a Gainesville homeowner can face, and the city's combination of reactive clay soils and aging pipe materials makes them a recurring issue rather than a rare event. Cooke County's Vertisol clay swells when the spring rains arrive and shrinks again during summer dry spells, and that repeated movement exerts shear stress on every copper or galvanized pipe embedded in a concrete slab.

How galvanized pipe accelerates the problem

Homes built near Downtown Gainesville and throughout the established residential neighbourhoods between the 1940s and 1970s were typically plumbed with galvanized steel, a material that corrodes from the inside out over decades. Internal scale buildup reduces pipe diameter and wall thickness simultaneously, so by the time seasonal soil movement applies lateral stress to an already-weakened section, a pinhole or hairline fracture is the predictable result. A standard visual inspection cannot find that failure because the pipe runs inside a concrete slab or behind a finished wall — a water leak detector based on electronic amplification or acoustic ground-microphone technology is required to isolate the leak point without opening the floor.

Plumbing Leak Detection for Gainesville Homes on Well Water

Plumbing leak detection for a well water system requires a different starting point than detection on a municipal supply. City-connected homes in Gainesville show a leak signature on their utility bill and experience a measurable drop in line pressure; a well-supplied home has no utility meter to flag the anomaly, so a failing pressure tank, a cracked well casing, or a buried supply-line fracture can drain significant water into the clay soil before anyone notices. Water sensing equipment placed at the pressure tank and along the distribution lines gives our team the equivalent of a meter reading and identifies where in the system pressure is being lost.

Detection on well systems versus municipal supply

On a municipal supply line, we can close the main shutoff and perform a static pressure hold test to confirm whether the in-home system is losing pressure, then use acoustic amplification to walk the slab or yard and identify the leak point. On a well water system, the pressure tank itself must be tested first — a waterlogged or failed tank mimics the pressure signature of a supply-line leak and needs to be ruled out before the buried lines are scanned. Once the tank is confirmed sound, acoustic and electronic leak detection proceeds along the distribution runs in the same way as a city-connected property.
